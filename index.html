Genesis

In 1990, at EUROCRYPT in Aarhus, Xuejia Lai and James L. Massey of ETH Zürich proposed a new block cipher. They called it PES, the Proposed Encryption Standard.

A year later they strengthened the round against differential cryptanalysis, which Eli Biham and Adi Shamir had just published. The new version was called IPES (Improved PES) and was later renamed IDEA.

Phil Zimmermann chose it for PGP 2.0, so for years PGP encrypted e-mail with IDEA. It was patented, and commercial users paid Ascom for a licence until the European patent ran out on 16 May 2011 and the American one on 7 January 2012.

IDEA has no S-boxes and no lookup tables. It works on 16-bit words, combines them with three operations, and cuts the key into 52 subkeys. Sections II–IV go through each part.

  • 1990 PES presented at EUROCRYPT in Aarhus
  • 1991 IPES, later renamed IDEA
  • PGP 2.0 encrypts mail with IDEA
  • 1993 a class of 251 weak keys (Daemen, Govaerts, Vandewalle)
  • 2011–12 patents expire (EU 2011, US 2012)
  • 2012 biclique attack on the full 8½ rounds: 2126.1

Three
operations

IDEA works on 16-bit words and combines them with three operations, each from a different algebraic group. None of them distributes over another or associates with another, so formulas that mix them do not simplify.

Exclusive or

Bit by bit: equal bits give 0, different give 1. Applying the same word twice undoes it.

⊕=AAAA

Addition mod 216

Ordinary addition, the carry out of bit 15 thrown away. Every word has a negative.

⊞=0001

Multiplication mod 216+1

65537 is prime, so every word has an inverse. The word 0 stands for 216, which makes the operation a bijection on all 65536 words.

The round

The 64-bit block is split into four 16-bit words, X1…X4. Four of the round's six subkeys are applied to them directly. Then the MA-structure (multiply–add) takes two XORs of those words, combines them with the last two subkeys, and its two outputs are XORed back into all four words.

At the end of a round the two middle words swap places. After eight rounds, a half round (the output transformation) undoes the last swap and applies four more subkeys.

out11FB ED2B 0198 6DE5

Defaults are the worked example from the IDEA description. Expected output: 11FB ED2B 0198 6DE5.

Key
schedule

The 128-bit key is split into eight 16-bit words, Z1…Z8. Then the whole key is rotated left by 25 bits and split again into Z9…Z16. Six rotations give the 52 subkeys: six per round and four for the output transformation. Hover a subkey to see which key bits it came from.

Inversion

Decryption runs the same rounds. Only the subkeys change: they are taken in reverse order, and

  • keys used with ⊙ become their inverses modulo 65537;
  • keys used with ⊞ become their negatives modulo 65536, and in rounds 2–8 the two of them swap places, because the middle words were swapped;
  • the MA-structure keys stay as they are: its output is XORed in twice, and XOR undoes itself.

So the same code (or chip) does both directions. Switch the round diagram above to decrypt and give it the ciphertext to see it.

Wounds

  1. Weak keys (251). Daemen, Govaerts and Vandewalle (CRYPTO ’93) found a class of 251 keys that a chosen-plaintext attacker can detect in use. Later work found larger weak-key classes. Out of 2128 keys, a randomly chosen one is practically never weak.

  2. Narrow bicliques (2126.1). Khovratovich, Leurent and Rechberger (EUROCRYPT 2012) recovered the key of the full 8½ rounds about two bits faster than brute force. A theoretical result, not a practical attack.

  3. 64-bit block size (232). After about 232 blocks (32 GiB) under one key, ciphertext blocks start to collide (birthday paradox), and collisions leak plaintext. Bhargavan and Leurent showed it in practice against 64-bit ciphers in TLS and OpenVPN in 2016 (Sweet32). This, not the attacks above, is the practical reason to avoid 64-bit block ciphers today.

No practical break of the full cipher with a random key is known. The file tool below avoids the 232-block limit by deriving a fresh key for every file.

The angel
in ECB

modes of operation

IDEA encrypts 8 bytes at a time. A mode of operation decides how a longer message is fed to it. ECB encrypts every block on its own, so equal blocks give equal ciphertext. CBC XORs each block with the previous ciphertext block first. CTR encrypts a counter and XORs the result into the data, which turns IDEA into a stream cipher. A sealed file is more than plain IDEA. IDEA alone does not notice tampering: anyone could flip bits in the file. And one key for every file would run into the 232-block limit from Wounds. So:

  • a random 8-byte IV is drawn for every file;
  • HKDF-SHA256, salted with that IV, turns your 128-bit key into a fresh IDEA key and a separate MAC key;
  • the file name and contents are encrypted with IDEA in CBC or CTR mode;
  • HMAC-SHA256 covers the header and the ciphertext. When opening, the tag is checked before any plaintext is used.
